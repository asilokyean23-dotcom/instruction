The basics 🌷

Tirzepatide copies two natural hormones from your gut. It helps your body control blood sugar and feel full sooner. Doctors prescribe it for type 2 diabetes and, with certain brands, for weight management.

Your dose, step by step 💗

Everyone starts with a small dose and goes up slowly. Tap each step to see what it means. You only move up when your doctor says so.

Increases happen in 2.5 mg steps, at least 4 weeks apart. Many people stay on a lower dose long term, and that is fine. The right dose depends on your condition and how you respond.

How to inject 🎀

Your pen comes with illustrated Instructions for Use. Ask your pharmacist or nurse to watch you do the first injection.

  1. Get readyTake the pen out of the fridge. Check the label, the dose and the expiry date. The liquid should be clear and colorless to slightly yellow. Do not use it if it is cloudy or has particles.
  2. Wash your hands and pick a spotUse your belly, thigh or the back of your upper arm. Choose a different spot from last week. Keep at least a few centimeters from your last site. Avoid skin that is bruised, tender or scarred.
  3. Clean the skinWipe with an alcohol swab and let it dry.
  4. Inject as the device instructsFollow your pen’s steps for unlocking, pressing and holding. Keep it in place for as long as the instructions say so the full dose goes in.
  5. Dispose safelyPut the used pen in a sharps container, or a hard plastic bottle with a lid if you don’t have one. Never reuse a pen or share it with anyone.
  6. Write it downNote the date, dose and site. It makes follow-up visits and missed-dose decisions much easier.

Storing your pens

Keep them in the fridge and never freeze them. The label allows a limited time at room temperature if needed, so check your product’s Instructions for Use for the exact limit. Keep pens away from heat and direct sun.

The label’s rule: take a missed dose as soon as possible within 4 days (96 hours). After that, skip it. Never double up. You can change your injection day if needed, as long as two doses are at least 3 days (72 hours) apart.

Side effects and warning signs 🩷

Most side effects are tummy troubles. They are usually worst in the first weeks and after a dose increase, and they often get easier.

Common and usually manageable

  • Nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting
  • Stomach discomfort, less appetite
  • Tiredness, mild injection-site redness

Smaller meals, eating slowly, avoiding greasy food and drinking plenty of fluids often help. Tell your prescriber if symptoms don’t settle.

Get medical help promptly

  • Severe stomach pain that doesn’t go away, with or without vomiting
  • Repeated vomiting or signs of dehydration
  • Swelling of face, lips or throat, or trouble breathing
  • A lump or swelling in the neck, hoarseness, trouble swallowing
  • Shakiness, sweating or confusion if you also use insulin or a sulfonylurea

Who should not use it

Tirzepatide carries a boxed warning for thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rats. It must not be used by anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2, or by anyone allergic to it. Do not combine it with any other tirzepatide product or any GLP-1 medicine unless a clinician directs it.

About retatrutide 🔍

You may have heard about retatrutide. It is a newer medicine from the same company as tirzepatide, and it is not approved yet, so this section explains where it stands instead of how to use it.

Not approved anywhere and still being tested. The maker plans to apply to the FDA in early 2027. Until then there is no official dose and no safe way to use it on your own.

What studies report so far

  • Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and stomach pain were the most common side effects
  • Results come from research settings with doctors watching closely
  • Long-term safety is not known yet

Why there is no dose chart here

  • No official dose or schedule exists, because it is not approved
  • The FDA says it cannot be made by compounding pharmacies
  • Products sold online as “research” retatrutide are not checked for purity or strength, and fakes exist
  • It should never be mixed with tirzepatide or any other GLP-1 medicine

What you can do instead

If you are curious about retatrutide, ask your doctor whether a clinical trial near you is a good fit. Trials are listed on ClinicalTrials.gov, and they provide the medicine and monitoring for free. In the meantime, tirzepatide is the approved medicine with a proven dosing plan, and the sections above cover it.

About GHK-Cu 🧴

GHK-Cu is often called a “copper peptide.” It is very different from tirzepatide, and it mostly belongs in skincare, not in weight-loss treatment. Here is the simple picture.

GHK-Cu is a copper peptide used in some face creams and serums. It is not an FDA-approved medicine, and it is not a partner or replacement for tirzepatide.

If you want to try it on your skin

  • Choose a cream or serum from a trusted skincare brand
  • Follow the instructions on the product label
  • Test a small spot first and wait a day for any redness
  • Stop if your skin burns, itches or swells
  • Ask a doctor first if you are pregnant, have a copper-related condition, or have sensitive skin

Why there is no injection guide here

  • Injected GHK-Cu is not approved for any use
  • The FDA has raised safety concerns about injectable versions, and there is very little human research
  • Whether pharmacies may legally make it is unclear and changing
  • Vials sold online as “research” products are not checked for purity, sterility or strength

💗 A note on sourcing. Only get tirzepatide on prescription from a licensed pharmacy. Products sold online as “research” peptides are not checked for purity or dose, and counterfeit weight-loss injections are a documented problem.
